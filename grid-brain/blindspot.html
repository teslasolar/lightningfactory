THE BLIND SPOT

Why neuroscience can't see this

01The Problem

Every EEG lab in the world runs on grid power. Every brain scan happens inside a building filled with 50 or 60 Hz electromagnetic fields. And what do researchers do with the 50/60 Hz signal in their data?

They filter it out.

A "notch filter" at the local grid frequency is standard practice in every EEG system. It's considered artifact removal — cleaning up the signal. But this creates a fundamental blind spot: if the brain's activity at exactly the grid frequency is being influenced by the grid, the notch filter removes the evidence.

02The Notch Filter

EEG amplifiers pick up electrical noise from the environment. The largest source is the power grid — a strong, stable signal at exactly 50 or 60 Hz. To see the brain's "real" signals, researchers apply a notch filter that removes a narrow band around the grid frequency.

This is completely reasonable from an engineering perspective. The grid signal is orders of magnitude stronger than the brain signal at that frequency. Without the filter, it would dominate the recording.

The catch: A notch filter can't distinguish between grid noise picked up by the electrodes and actual neural activity at the same frequency. If the brain oscillates at 50 or 60 Hz — whether spontaneously or in response to the ambient field — that activity gets deleted along with the noise.

03What We Can't Measure

This creates several blind spots in neuroscience:

1. Entrainment: If neural oscillations entrain (synchronize) to the grid frequency, the notch filter removes the evidence. We literally cannot detect brain-grid coupling with standard EEG methods.

2. Baseline shifts: If constant grid exposure shifts the brain's baseline activity at 50/60 Hz, every experiment ever conducted has been done with this shift already present — it looks "normal" because we've never measured a brain without it.

3. Cross-frequency effects: The grid's harmonics (100, 120, 150, 180 Hz...) could affect higher brain rhythms. These are typically filtered out as well.

Present

05What Would It Take?

To actually test whether the grid affects brain activity, you'd need:

1. A shielded room — a Faraday cage that blocks external EMF, with battery-powered EEG equipment inside.

2. Comparison recordings — same subjects, same tasks, with and without grid-frequency fields present.

3. No notch filter — record the raw signal and use spatial filtering (like beamforming) to separate brain sources from environmental sources.

This experiment is entirely feasible. It hasn't been done because the assumption that the grid is "just noise" is so deeply embedded in the field that questioning it doesn't occur to most researchers.
